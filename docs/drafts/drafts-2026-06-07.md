```
Nice to see C2PA and DID treated as two halves of one problem instead of
competing standards — most write-ups pick one and wave at the other.

The thing I keep getting stuck on, and I'd like to know how Vouch handles it:
C2PA binds a signature to an artifact (this manifest signed these bytes) and a
DID binds to an actor (this is the agent). The interesting part is the join. When
a verifier has a C2PA manifest whose signer resolves to a DID, what's actually
trusting what at that seam — does the manifest's signing cert chain to a key in
the DID document, or are the two asserted side by side and the verifier correlates
them after the fact? That join is where I'd expect a forgery to try to hide, so
I'm curious where you put the trust anchor.

Second question, on lifecycle: agents rotate and get compromised more often than
people do. If an agent's key is revoked or rotated after it signed something, what
does verification of the older artifact return? "Valid as of signing time" and
"signer no longer trusted" are very different answers, and I couldn't tell from
the README which one a verifier gives.

For context on why I'm asking: I've been working the artifact side of this rather
than the identity side — a small file format (.win) whose verifier returns a fixed
set of states: a valid container verifies, a tampered payload reports Tampered, a
tampered signature reports Invalid, a truncated container reports Damaged. I'll be
straight about where it stands. Those states are backed by the project's own
integration tests passing on a clean checkout (latest run: 323 tests, 0 failures,
stable Rust 1.94.1 on macOS), but that's first-party and single-machine — no
outside reviewer has audited it, and I haven't proven cryptographic soundness or
anything beyond the inputs my test corpus encodes. So I'm reading your DID layer
partly as the piece I deliberately left out, which is why the revocation question
is a live one for me too. Bookmarked it — good luck with the launch.
```
